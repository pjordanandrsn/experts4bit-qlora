The lighthouse keeper kept two logbooks. The first recorded what the instruments said: the barometer at dawn and dusk, the direction of the wind, the height of the swell against the third rung of the landing ladder, and the hour at which each ship's light rose over the horizon and set again behind the headland. The second recorded what he had expected the instruments to say, written the evening before, in a smaller hand, before he went down to trim the wick. Most nights the two books agreed to within a few hundredths of an inch of mercury and a point or two of the compass. On the nights they did not, he did not correct either book. He drew a line under the entry, wrote the difference in the margin, and the next morning he walked the length of the causeway to read the gauge at the harbour office, because a single instrument that disagrees with a forecast tells you only that something moved, and he wanted to know whether it was the weather, the barometer, or the man who had written the forecast. Over eleven winters the margin filled with small numbers. Most of them came from the barometer, whose tube had a flaw that caught the mercury on cold mornings; a few came from his own habit of rounding the wind to the nearest quarter; and three, which he underlined twice, came from storms that no one on the coast had seen coming.
